Chapter 3: Laws of Motion

Chapter 3: Laws of Motion

Classical mechanics, and Newton's Laws in particular, form the bedrock of physics as we know it. In this chapter, we explore the three fundamental laws that govern the motion of any object in the universe — from a falling apple to orbiting satellites.

3.1 Newton's First Law — The Law of Inertia

Newton's First Law states that a body at rest remains at rest, and a body in motion continues to move in a straight line at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force. This property of matter — the tendency to resist any change in its state of motion — is called inertia.

The word "inertia" comes from the Latin word meaning "idleness" or "sluggishness". Heavier objects have more inertia and are harder to set in motion or stop.
F_net = 0 ⟹ a = 0 (velocity is constant or zero)

A common misconception is that a moving body needs a continuous force to maintain its motion. This was Aristotle's view, and it is incorrect. Newton showed that no force is needed to maintain uniform motion — only to change it.

3.2 Newton's Second Law — The Law of Acceleration

Newton's Second Law quantifies how a force changes the motion of a body. It states that the net force acting on a body is equal to the rate of change of its linear momentum. For a body with constant mass, this simplifies to the familiar equation.

F = ma
F = dp/dt = d(mv)/dt

This law introduces the concept of acceleration — the rate of change of velocity. The SI unit of force, the Newton (N), is defined as the force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s².

The Second Law is the most general of the three laws and encompasses the First Law as a special case (when F = 0, a = 0).

3.3 Newton's Third Law — Action and Reaction

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. When body A exerts a force on body B, body B simultaneously exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on body A. These forces always act on different bodies.

F_AB = −F_BA

A critical point often missed: action-reaction pairs never cancel each other out because they act on different objects. They would only cancel if they acted on the same object, which they never do in a true action-reaction pair.

3.4 Applications — Free Body Diagrams

A Free Body Diagram (FBD) isolates a single object and shows all the forces acting on it. Drawing a proper FBD is the most important skill in mechanics problems. Identify every surface in contact, every field force, and resolve all forces into components along chosen axes.

For JEE problems involving pulleys, inclined planes, and connected bodies — always draw separate FBDs for each object. Newton's Second Law is then applied to each body individually.

3.5 Friction

Friction is the resistive force that opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact. There are two main types: static friction (which prevents the onset of motion) and kinetic friction (which acts during relative motion).

f_s ≤ μ_s × N (static friction — can vary)
f_k = μ_k × N (kinetic friction — constant)

The coefficient of friction μ depends on the materials of the surfaces in contact, not on the area of contact or the speed of motion (for kinetic friction).
